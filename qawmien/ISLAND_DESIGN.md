# The Beginner Island — design brief

The owner asked for a **floating beginner island**, researched properly, with **its own theme**
rather than a copy of Dofus. Everything from the island through to the ruin should feel continuous.

## What Dofus actually does (the reference, not the target)

Incarnam is a floating island above the world, concept by Etienne Corbaux. What matters for us is
its **structure**, not its art:

- One island in the sky, and deliberately **simplified** over time — Ankama *removed* the outlying
  islets around its edge because they diluted it.
- The island is split into **5 named sub-zones**, each with its own look and its own monster
  family: the entrance, Prairie (meadow), Forêt (forest), Lac (lake), Cimetière (cemetery).
- Each zone is a handful of map-boxes. The cemetery is the most visited because it has the best
  XP — i.e. zones are differentiated by **reward**, not just by scenery.

The lesson: a small island reads as big when it is divided into places that look and play
differently. Fifteen interchangeable grass screens read as one screen shown fifteen times.

## Our theme — Maltese limestone, not Breton fantasy

KARTI is a Maltese game, and Malta has something no fantasy setting can borrow: the **oldest
free-standing stone temples on earth**. That is our theme, and it is genuinely distinct from
Dofus's soft green French countryside.

**The island:** a slab of golden Maltese limestone torn out of the sea and left hanging, with the
Mediterranean far below instead of clouds. Warm honey stone, sun-bleached and salt-worn.

- **Palette:** honey limestone, dry gold grass, prickly-pear green, deep Mediterranean blue below,
  and the game's existing violet for anything arcane. Warm and bright, so the ruin's cold torchlit
  interior contrasts when you step inside.
- **Underside:** the island is TORN, not cut — raw rock, hanging roots, a few smaller fragments
  drifting in its shadow. Seen from the map screen and at the world's rim.
- **The rim:** where the grid ends, the ground ends. Cliff edge, then sky and sea. No invisible
  wall, no water tile pretending to be a boundary — the edge of the world should look like an edge.
- **Landmarks:** terraced field walls (rubble walls, the *ħitan tas-sejjieħ*), prickly pear, a
  wind-bent carob tree, salt pans catching light, and the megalithic ruin the player wakes in.

**Continuity to the ruin, which the owner called out specifically:** the ruin is not a separate
tileset that happens to sit nearby — it is the **same limestone**, older and fallen. Walking out of
the gate should read as walking out of a temple onto the island it was carved from: same stone,
same light, interior cold and torchlit, exterior warm and open.

## Zones — 15 field maps, five places

Following Incarnam's structure with our own content:

| zone | maps | look | feel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Temple Approach** | 3 | cracked pavement, fallen columns, dry grass | where you arrive; safest |
| **The Terraces** | 3 | rubble field walls, crops gone wild, prickly pear | open, gentle |
| **The Carob Wood** | 3 | wind-bent trees, dappled shade, deeper green | cover, ambush |
| **Salt Pans** | 3 | shallow water in cut squares, bright reflections | exposed, pretty |
| **The Necropolis** | 3 | tombs cut into rock, bone-dry, cold light | best rewards, hardest |

The Necropolis is deliberately the Cimetière lesson: the most dangerous zone should be the one
worth going to.

## What this changes

1. The world's rim becomes an island edge (cliff → sky/sea), not water or a fence.
2. Ground gains five distinct materials rather than one grass.
3. The world map draws an island, not a rectangle — the shape itself becomes recognisable.
4. Encounters differ by zone, which is what makes the map worth crossing. **This is content, not
   art, and it is the piece that is currently missing entirely** — 11 of 15 field maps have
   nothing on them today.
